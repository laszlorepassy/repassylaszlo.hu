teacher of informatics and mathematics

SOCRATES: Do not think that I only want to split hairs, but I feel that we have not yet found the answer to your question. Both of us now see more clearly than when we sat down here what mathematics is, and what sort of work the mathematician does; yet to the question of what its purpose and meaning are, we have in truth given no answer at all.

HIPPOCRATES: Thinking it over, you are right. It has already set my mind at rest to understand why the study of mathematics can lead me to certain knowledge. I feel that exploring this wonderful world would give me the contentment I have so far found in nothing else: that there is something I have no reason to doubt. And now that I have also understood that the world of mathematics exists in its own way — not as stones and trees do, but no less independently of me — I am more at ease still. But truly: what is the purpose of exploring this world? Would we not reach our goal soonest if, just this once, you set your method aside and answered the question yourself? For I feel that I am not able to.

SOCRATES: Even if I could answer it on my own, I would not, for it would do you little good. We understand only what we work out for ourselves; what we are handed ready-made, without labouring for it in our souls, goes in one ear and out the other. It is like watering plants: a plant cannot live without water, but it gains little from water poured over its leaves, which merely runs off; the only water it can truly use is the water it draws up itself, through its roots.

— Alfréd Rényi: Dialogues on Mathematics (excerpt translated from the Hungarian)
